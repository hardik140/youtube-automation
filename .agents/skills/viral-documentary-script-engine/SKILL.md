# VIRAL DOCUMENTARY SCRIPT ENGINE
## Personalized Claude Skill for Hardik

> Purpose: Automate the research-to-scriptwriting layer of video production while preserving Hardik's own voice, personality, curiosity, ambition, and practical/technical mindset. This skill is designed to produce high-retention documentary, explainer, investigation, business, technology, AI, startup, FMCG, and educational video scripts.
>
> Core principle: Do not copy any creator's wording, personality, catchphrases, or recognizable style. Learn and reuse the underlying storytelling mechanics: curiosity, contrast, escalation, evidence, human stakes, open loops, re-hooks, and payoff.

---

# 1. WHO THIS SKILL IS FOR

The user is Hardik.

Use the following non-sensitive working profile when shaping scripts:

- Engineering/technology background with strong interest in AI, Agentic AI, RAG, automation, software, startups, product building, and business.
- Hands-on builder rather than a purely theoretical creator.
- Interested in FMCG/manufacturing and practical business problems as well as technology.
- Likes understanding systems: how something works, why it fails, who benefits, what can be improved, and how to build a solution.
- Prefers practical, actionable explanations over abstract academic language.
- Comfortable with Hindi/Hinglish and English.
- Content should feel like a smart young Indian builder/researcher explaining something to another curious person.
- Voice should be confident, direct, curious, analytical, conversational, and occasionally humorous.
- Avoid sounding like a professor, corporate PR person, motivational guru, or artificially aggressive influencer.
- The audience should feel that Hardik is discovering, testing, explaining, and connecting the dots with them.
- Hardik's strongest natural positioning: "builder + investigator + explainer."

Do not invent personal experiences, credentials, investigations, interviews, locations, or claims for Hardik. If a first-person experience is not provided, write in an honest third-person or research-led form.

# 2. PRIMARY GOAL

Create scripts that maximize:

1. Scroll-stopping power
2. Watch-time
3. Retention
4. Curiosity
5. Emotional engagement
6. Information value
7. Shareability
8. Comment/discussion potential
9. Credibility
10. Memorability

The goal is NOT simply to put many facts into a script.

The goal is:

> "Make the viewer need the next sentence."

Every major section should either:
- answer a question,
- create a new question,
- raise the stakes,
- reveal something unexpected,
- introduce evidence,
- humanize the issue,
- or reframe what the viewer previously believed.

# 3. CORE VIRAL DOCUMENTARY FORMULA

Use this as the default architecture:

HOOK
→ CONTRADICTION
→ OPEN LOOP
→ PROMISE
→ MINIMUM CONTEXT
→ FIRST REVEAL
→ "BUT..."
→ NEW QUESTION
→ INVESTIGATION / EVIDENCE
→ HUMAN STORY
→ ESCALATION
→ SECOND REVEAL
→ SYSTEMIC EXPLANATION
→ CONSEQUENCE
→ FINAL REFRAME
→ PAYOFF
→ OPTIONAL CTA

Compressed formula:

> HOOK → CONTRAST → QUESTION → JOURNEY → DISCOVERY → BUT → NEW QUESTION → EVIDENCE → HUMAN STAKES → ESCALATION → REVEAL → REFRAME → CONSEQUENCE

Never force every element if the topic does not support it. The structure is a tool, not a cage.

---

# 4. THE MOST IMPORTANT WRITING PRINCIPLE

Do NOT begin by explaining the topic.

Weak:
"Today we are going to discuss India's ice cream industry."

Better:
"India's ice cream market looks simple from the outside. You buy a ₹10 product, eat it, and that's it. But behind that ₹10 price is a chain of margins, distributors, retailers, manufacturing costs, cold storage, and one brutal question: who is actually making money?"

The second version creates:
- context
- tension
- curiosity
- a business question

Always prefer:
> Reaction / surprising claim → mystery → explanation

over:
> Explanation → explanation → explanation

# 5. HOOK ENGINE

Generate 3-5 hook candidates before writing the full script.

Score every hook from 1-10 on:
- Curiosity
- Surprise
- Stakes
- Specificity
- Relevance
- Visual potential
- Novelty

Choose the strongest.

## Hook Type A — Contradiction

Formula:
> "Everyone thinks X. But reality is Y."

Examples:
- "India's cheapest food may be hiding its most expensive problem."
- "The technology designed to save companies time is creating a new kind of work."

Do not copy these examples literally.

## Hook Type B — Mystery

> "Something about X doesn't make sense."

Then delay the answer.

## Hook Type C — Famous thing + unknown fact

> "You know X. But you probably don't know what happened behind Y."

## Hook Type D — Extreme specificity

Use a surprising number, date, price, distance, percentage, or scale.

Only use verified numbers.

## Hook Type E — Personal relevance

> "You probably use/buy/watch X. Here's the part you don't see."

## Hook Type F — Challenge a common assumption

> "If you think X means Y, there's a problem."

Do not manufacture controversy.

## Hook Type G — Story opening

Start in the middle of a high-stakes moment.

## Hook Type H — Question

A question must be genuinely interesting and preferably difficult to answer immediately.

## Hook Type I — Human consequence

Start with one person's situation, then expand to the larger system.

## Hook Type J — Investigation

> "I wanted to understand why X keeps happening, so I started looking at Y."

Only use first-person investigation if it is true.

---

# 6. THREE-LAYER HOOK

For important videos, try to build:

Layer 1 — Emotional:
> "Wait, what?"

Layer 2 — Intellectual:
> "How is that possible?"

Layer 3 — Narrative:
> "I need to know what caused it."

A strong opening makes the viewer experience all three.

---

# 7. OPEN LOOP SYSTEM

An open loop is an unanswered question.

Examples of loop types:

- Why does this happen?
- Who benefits?
- Who failed?
- What happened next?
- Is the common explanation wrong?
- What is the hidden business model?
- What happens behind the scenes?
- Why hasn't the obvious solution worked?
- What changes if this continues?
- What does the evidence actually show?

Rules:
1. Open a loop early.
2. Do not answer it immediately.
3. Give partial answers.
4. Close the loop with a satisfying payoff.
5. Open the next loop before closing the current one when appropriate.

Use a "question ladder":

Question 1
→ answer
→ Question 2
→ answer
→ Question 3
→ bigger reveal

This is one of the primary retention engines.

---

# 8. CONTRAST ENGINE

High-retention stories often use:

Expectation → Reality

Useful contrast pairs:
- Beautiful → horrifying
- Safe → dangerous
- Cheap → hidden cost
- Famous → unknown truth
- Simple → complex
- Legal → questionable
- Powerful → vulnerable
- Efficient → wasteful
- Innovation → unintended consequence
- Promise → reality
- Public story → hidden story
- Individual failure → systemic failure

Find the strongest natural contrast in every topic.

Never manufacture a contradiction where evidence does not support one.

---

# 9. THE "BUT" ENGINE

Use "but" as narrative friction.

Pattern:

> X happened.
> But...
> Y changes the meaning of X.

Other transition concepts:
- "लेकिन असली सवाल..."
- "पर कहानी यहां बदलती है..."
- "और यहीं चीज interesting हो जाती है..."
- "लेकिन एक problem है..."
- "अब सवाल है..."
- "और यही हमें अगले सवाल तक ले जाता है..."

Do not overuse the exact same phrase. Vary the language.

---

# 10. MICRO-HOOKS / RE-HOOKS

Long videos need multiple attention resets.

Insert a meaningful re-hook approximately every 30-90 seconds depending on pacing.

Examples of functions:
- introduce a surprising fact
- raise a new question
- reveal a contradiction
- switch location/person/scale
- introduce evidence
- preview a major reveal
- change emotional temperature

Do NOT insert empty clickbait phrases such as "you won't believe what happened next" unless the following information genuinely justifies it.

Every re-hook must earn its place.

---

# 11. RETENTION MAP

Before finalizing a long script, create a private retention map:

0:00 Hook
0:15 Core question
0:30 Promise
1:00 Context
2:00 First reveal
3:00 New question
4:00 Evidence
5:00 Human story
6:00 Escalation
7:00 Bigger reveal
...
Final: Reframe/payoff

The exact timestamps depend on target length.

For every segment ask:

> "Why would someone keep watching from this point?"

If there is no strong answer, rewrite or compress the section.

---

# 12. INFORMATION DENSITY VS CURIOSITY DENSITY

Do not confuse:
- Information density = how many facts are presented.
- Curiosity density = how often the viewer has a reason to continue.

A script can have 100 facts and be boring.

Prefer:
> fact → question → answer → surprise → new question

over:
> fact → fact → fact → fact

# 13. CONVERSATIONAL VOICE

Hardik's default voice:

- conversational
- intelligent
- direct
- young
- curious
- practical
- confident without pretending certainty
- occasionally humorous
- Indian context where relevant
- Hinglish when it improves naturalness

Preferred:
"देखो, problem यहां से शुरू होती है."

"अब एक चीज समझो."

"लेकिन यहां एक twist है."

"और यही सबसे interesting part है."

Avoid excessive:
"दोस्तों आज की इस वीडियो में हम जानेंगे..."

Avoid generic motivational filler.

Avoid sounding like:
- news anchor
- school textbook
- corporate presentation
- fake guru
- rage-bait influencer

---

# 14. SENTENCE RHYTHM

Use variable sentence length.

For explanation:
Longer, clear sentences.

For impact:
Short.

Examples of rhythm:
"सब ठीक लग रहा था.

फिर एक चीज बदली.

और यहीं से पूरी कहानी बदल गई."

Use pauses strategically.

Do not make every sentence short. Constant short sentences become artificial.

---

# 15. WORD CHOICE

Prefer concrete words.

Weak:
"This was a significant issue."

Strong:
"एक छोटी सी pricing decision ने पूरी margin structure बदल दी."

Prefer:
- specific nouns
- strong verbs
- concrete numbers
- sensory descriptions
- memorable metaphors

Avoid unnecessary adjectives.

---

# 16. METAPHOR ENGINE

When a complicated system needs to be remembered, create a simple mental model.

Example pattern:
> "इसे ऐसे समझो जैसे..."

Use metaphors to compress complexity.

A metaphor should:
- simplify
- create a visual image
- remain accurate
- help the viewer remember the argument

Never use a metaphor that distorts the facts.

---

# 17. HUMANIZATION ENGINE

Large issues should periodically become human.

Abstract:
"India loses billions due to inefficiency."

Human:
"अब सोचो उस छोटे business owner पर, जिसके लिए यही ₹20,000 महीने की cash-flow gap salary रोक सकती है."

Only use hypothetical examples when clearly framed as examples. Prefer real documented people when available.

Pattern:
MACRO
→ SYSTEM
→ PERSON
→ CONSEQUENCE
→ MACRO

---

# 18. SCALE SWITCHING

Avoid staying at one scale.

Use:
India
→ city
→ company
→ factory
→ worker/customer
→ system
→ India

This creates narrative movement.

---

# 19. EVIDENCE LADDER

For investigative/educational content:

1. Claim
2. Observation
3. Specific example
4. Primary source/document/data
5. Expert interpretation
6. Counterpoint
7. Synthesis

Whenever possible prioritize:
- primary documents
- official statistics
- academic research
- original datasets
- regulatory filings
- court documents
- direct interviews
- credible journalism

Do not treat a viral post as proof.

---

# 20. CREDIBILITY RULE

Virality is worthless if the audience stops trusting the creator.

Therefore:
- Never invent statistics.
- Never invent quotes.
- Never invent interviews.
- Never fabricate personal experiences.
- Never state allegations as facts.
- Distinguish fact, inference, allegation, and opinion.
- Add uncertainty where evidence is uncertain.
- If sources disagree, explain the disagreement.

A strong script can say:
"The evidence is mixed."

That can increase credibility.

---

# 21. SOURCE PLACEMENT

Do not overload the spoken narration with citations.

Instead:
- keep narration natural
- add source references in production notes/on-screen graphics/description where appropriate
- mention the source verbally when its authority itself matters

Pattern:
Story → evidence → source → interpretation → consequence.

---

# 22. EMOTIONAL TEMPERATURE

Do not maintain one emotion for the entire video.

A useful sequence:
Curiosity
→ surprise
→ humor
→ concern
→ anger
→ empathy
→ explanation
→ hope / clarity

Not every video needs all of them.

The objective is emotional variation, not emotional manipulation.

---

# 23. MORAL QUESTION ENGINE

Strong topics often contain a deeper question:

- Who is responsible?
- Why did the system allow this?
- Who benefits?
- Why does this continue?
- Is the solution actually solving the problem?
- What are we misunderstanding?
- What happens if nothing changes?

Use moral questions carefully. Do not turn complex issues into simplistic villains.

---

# 24. OPINION MOMENTS

A good educational video gives the viewer something to think about.

Create moments where the viewer can reasonably ask:
"Was this decision right?"
"Would you do the same?"
"Who should be responsible?"
"Is this actually a good business?"
"Does the evidence support the popular belief?"

Do not manufacture polarisation just for comments.

---

# 25. HUMOR

Use humor as a reset, not as the entire identity.

Good humor:
- observational
- self-aware
- situational
- relevant to the point

Avoid:
- punching down
- insensitive jokes around victims
- fake outrage
- constant sarcasm

---

# 26. VISUAL-FIRST WRITING

Every important spoken line should have a potential visual.

When writing, optionally add:

[VISUAL]
[B-ROLL]
[GRAPHIC]
[SCREEN RECORDING]
[MAP]
[ARCHIVAL]
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]
[PAUSE]
[SFX]

Example:

NARRATION:
"₹10 की ice cream बेचकर आखिर manufacturer कितना बचाता है?"

VISUAL:
Product → factory → distributor → retailer → margin breakdown.

This converts an abstract question into a visual journey.

Possible visual changes:
- talking head
- B-roll
- close-up
- screenshot
- document
- graph
- map
- animation
- archival footage
- product shot
- screen recording
- interview
- on-screen quote

Do not change visuals randomly. Change them when the information or emotional beat changes.

# 28. TITLE ENGINE

A title should create a promise/open loop.

Common structures:

1. The Hidden Cost of X
2. Why X Doesn't Work
3. The Truth Behind X
4. How X Really Works
5. What Nobody Tells You About X
6. The Business Behind X
7. Why Everyone Is Wrong About X
8. Inside X
9. The Problem With X
10. How X Became Y
11. X: The Part You Never See
12. I Investigated X

Do not use generic titles if a sharper promise exists.

Title and opening hook should complement each other.

Do not simply repeat the title in the first sentence.

---

# 29. THUMBNAIL CONCEPT ENGINE

A thumbnail should create a visual question, not explain the whole video.

Use:
- one dominant object/person
- one emotional cue
- 2-5 words maximum where possible
- strong contrast
- simple composition
- visual mystery

Do not make the thumbnail identical to the title.

---

# 30. CTA ENGINE

CTA should fit the video.

Avoid:
"Like, share, subscribe, comment, bell icon..." all at once.

Better:
- ask a genuine opinion
- ask one useful question
- point to the next related topic
- ask viewers to share an experience

CTA should not destroy the emotional ending.

---

# 31. ENDING ENGINE

Strong endings often follow:

Answer original question
→ zoom out
→ explain why it matters
→ final reframe
→ memorable line

Pattern:
"The problem looked like X.

But after looking at the evidence, the bigger problem is Y.

And that's why this matters."

Do not add a generic "So guys, that's it" immediately after a powerful conclusion.

---

# 32. REFRAME ENGINE

One of the strongest documentary payoffs:

> "At first, we thought the problem was X. But actually, the deeper problem is Y."

Examples of generic structures:
- not a technology problem → implementation problem
- not a pricing problem → distribution problem
- not an individual failure → system problem
- not lack of information → incentive problem

Only use a reframe when the evidence supports it.

---

# 33. TOPIC SELECTION FILTER

Before scripting, score the topic 1-10:

- Existing audience interest
- Curiosity
- Novelty
- Stakes
- Conflict
- Personal relevance
- Visual potential
- Evidence availability
- Human story potential
- Search/discovery potential
- Discussion potential
- Ability to create a satisfying payoff

If the topic has low curiosity, conflict, stakes, and visual potential, consider changing the angle rather than forcing the topic.

# 36. CHAPTER TRANSITION ENGINE

Good transition:
"We now know what happened. But that still doesn't explain why it happened."

Other functions:
- consequence → cause
- cause → beneficiary
- individual → system
- history → present
- problem → attempted solution
- attempted solution → unintended consequence

Transitions should move the investigation forward.

# 37. RETENTION FAILURE CHECKLIST

Rewrite if:
- opening starts with generic greetings
- first 20 seconds contain no meaningful curiosity
- too much background before the central question
- several paragraphs explain without changing the story
- statistics appear without meaning
- every section has the same emotional tone
- there are no human consequences
- there are no visual opportunities
- the conclusion merely repeats the introduction
- the CTA interrupts the payoff
- the script depends on fake controversy
- claims are stronger than the evidence

# 44. THE "NEXT SENTENCE" TEST

For every major paragraph ask:

> "If I stop here, does the viewer have a reason to hear the next sentence?"

If no:
- add a question
- add a reveal
- add a consequence
- compress the paragraph
- change the order
- or remove it

---

# 45. THE "WHY SHOULD I CARE?" TEST

Every major section should connect to:
- money
- time
- health/safety
- status
- opportunity
- risk
- identity
- relationships
- future
- society

Only use the relevant ones.

---

# 46. THE "SHOW, DON'T TELL" TEST

For every abstract sentence ask:

> "Can this be shown?"

If yes, suggest a visual.

Example:
Instead of:
"The market is fragmented."

Visual:
10 brands → distributors → retailers → price labels → margin graphic.

---

# 47. THE "ESCALATION" TEST

A good sequence should generally move:

Interesting
→ surprising
→ concerning
→ bigger
→ personal
→ systemic
→ consequential

Avoid revealing the strongest point too early unless the format specifically requires it.

---

# 48. THE "PAYOFF" TEST

The ending must pay off the opening.

If opening asks:
"How did this happen?"

Ending must answer:
"Here's how it happened."

If opening asks:
"Who benefits?"

Ending must reveal:
"Here's who benefits and why."

If opening asks:
"Is this actually true?"

Ending must resolve:
"Here's what the evidence supports."

# 53. EXAMPLE OF TRANSFORMING A BORING TOPIC

Topic:
"How ERP software works."

Bad:
"ERP stands for Enterprise Resource Planning. It helps companies manage..."

Hardik-style angle:
